Most New Zealand small businesses need a website before an app. Instagram and Facebook help people you already know. ## Do I need a website if I already have Instagram?

Yes, if you want people who do not already follow you to find you, trust you, and send an enquiry. Instagram is a channel. A website is an address you own.

Instagram is excellent at proof and personality. A Reel of Saturday brunch, a Story of a van on a job, a carousel of before-and-after work: that is how a lot of New Zealand businesses still get known. Keep doing that. The gap is what happens when someone is not already in your audience.

Search does not live inside your grid. A parent googling “plumber Lower Hutt after hours”, a tourist looking for “cafe near me that’s actually open”, a brand manager checking you exist before they reply to a DM: they land on whatever Google, Maps, or an AI overview can read. An Instagram profile is a poor substitute for that. It is logged-in, algorithm-sorted, and easy to miss if the app is closed.

A few practical limits of “Instagram is my website”:

- **You do not own the door.** Accounts get restricted. Features change. The link in bio is one slot, and it is a terrible place to hide hours, pricing, and a real enquiry form.
- **Google cannot treat a grid like a homepage.** Captions help a little. They are not a title tag, a service page, or a crawlable list of suburbs you cover.
- **Trust still wants a page.** Phone number, area served, GST-ready invoice path, who you are. A lot of buyers will not DM a stranger from a Reel.
- **Staff cannot update the business in one place.** Hours change. A Saturday special needs to live somewhere that is not a 24-hour Story.

Use Instagram for the people who already like you. Use a brand site for the people who are trying to find you. Point the bio at the site, not at another list of links, and let both do their jobs.

If you are a creator who only sells through DMs and never wants search traffic, you can wait. Most cafes, tradies, clinics, and local services should not.

## Website vs app — which first?

The website. Almost always.

An app is a product people keep on their home screen. That only pays off when they have a reason to come back: a booking they repeat, a login, points, a class timetable, a members’ hub, live ops. If you do not have that loop yet, an App Store listing is expensive theatre. You will still need a website so people can find you, read the offer, and install anything at all.

A simple split:

| You need… | Start with |
| --- | --- |
| To be found, believed, and emailed or called | A brand site |
| A menu, hours, services, suburbs, and an enquiry form | A brand site |
| Repeat bookings, a logged-in schedule, points or a simple rewards UI | A site with a login-ready shell, then consider an app |
| A game, holder hub, or live ops console | Custom, quoted per scope. Do not start in the App Store |

“Brand site” here means a site that is actually yours: your domain, your pages, your forms, your admin if you want it. It is not a Facebook page with a pretty cover photo, and it is not a native iPhone app.

When an app *does* come later, you still keep the website. Apple and Google both send people to a public page. Search still happens in a browser. Staff still need a place to change hours without a developer.

My Brand Studio’s [Business Site](https://mybrandstudio.club) package is the middle path a lot of owners actually need: a login-ready shell, schedule or hub patterns, points or simple rewards if that is the product, without pretending you must ship a native app in week one. Games, chain hooks, and full live-ops tooling are scoped separately. If that is you, say so on the discovery call. If you just need a cafe or tradie site that gets found, you do not need any of that yet.

## Website vs Linktree / Facebook page

A Linktree is a list. A Facebook page is a profile on someone else’s network. A website is a place.

Link-in-bio tools are fine as a temporary tray for a personal account. They are a weak home for a business. Every tap is another hop. There is no real “about”, no crawlable service page, no quiet place to put prices or a proper enquiry. Google has little to index except a stack of outbound buttons. If your whole online life is a Linktree, you are asking people to hunt.

A Facebook page is a bit closer to a site. You can post hours, a map pin, and updates. Plenty of local buyers still look there. Two problems remain: you do not own the platform, and a page is not a substitute for pages you can shape for search. Facebook decides distribution. It also decides whether your enquiry button still works the way it did last year.

A brand site gives you:

1. A URL you can print on a van, a coffee cup, and a GST invoice.
2. One enquiry form that arrives in a real inbox (or a routed one, if we automate that).
3. Pages Google and AI overviews can quote: services, suburbs, “do I need a website”, “what’s in the pie today”.
4. A bio link that goes somewhere finished, so Instagram and Facebook become doors, not the building.

Keep the Facebook page. Keep Instagram. Add the site as the place they both point to. If you already have a Linktree, we usually retire it as the homepage and keep it only if you truly need a pile of personal links.

## What “good enough” looks like in 2026

Good enough is not a 40-page brochure, a lottie explosion, or a chatbot that cannot take an enquiry. It is a fast page that tells the truth, can be found, and can be updated on a Tuesday without drama.

In 2026, a small NZ site that is actually doing its job usually has:

- **One job on the home page.** Who it is for, what you do, where you are, what to do next. Not five competing slogans.
- **A real enquiry path.** Form, phone, or booking. “Enquiry” on the button if you are talking to New Zealanders. The message has to arrive somewhere a human will see it.
- **Hours, area, and proof that look like you.** Photos of the actual room, the van, the bench. Not a stock marble counter in another country.
- **Mobile first, HTTPS, and a title that matches the search.** If someone types the name of the cafe, the cafe should appear as the cafe, not as “Home | Welcome to our site”.
- **A self-contained answer near the top of key pages.** People and AI overviews both skim. Put the answer in 40 to 60 words, then explain.
- **Something you can edit.** Saturday hours, a special, a new Reel URL. A light admin console beats paying a retainer to change a sentence.
- **Basic SEO foundations.** Indexable HTML, one idea per page, sensible headings, images with real alt text. Not a secret trick.

Good enough is allowed to be a one-page site. A same-day sketch at My Brand Studio *is* a one-page concept after a short call. Final page count is confirmed on that call. A cafe often needs home, menu, hours, and enquiry. A tradie often needs services, suburbs, a few jobs, and a form. A creator often needs a hub: who they are, where to watch, what is on this week.

What is *not* good enough in 2026:

- A builder template with “Lorem ipsum” still in the footer.
- A homepage made of screenshots of Instagram.
- A pop-up that begs for email before anyone knows who you are.
- An app-only presence with no public page.
- A Google Business Profile for a business that is not real.

If you can send a stranger the URL, and they can tell what you do and how to reach you in under a minute, you are in the right neighbourhood. We can then make it nicer, faster, and easier to find.

## How SEO actually works for a new site (no hacks)

SEO is not a switch. It is “can a search engine find a useful page, understand it, and show it when someone asks a matching question?” A new site starts with almost no history. Anyone who promises page one next week is selling you a story.

The unglamorous sequence:

1. **Make a page that answers a real question.** This page exists because people type “do I need a website” and similar phrases. Your service pages should exist because people type “florist Palmerston North” or “mobile mechanic Kapiti”, not because a tool spat out 50 keywords.
2. **Make it crawlable.** Real HTML, not a blank box that fills in after a script. HTTPS. A title and H1 that match. Headings that are questions if the search is a question. Internal links that a person would actually click.
3. **Put the business facts where humans and machines can reuse them.** Name, address or service area, phone, hours, enquiry. Same details on the site as on any real Google Business Profile.
4. **Earn the rest slowly.** Photos, a few genuinely useful pages, mentions from real people, a Maps listing *if you are a real local business*. Not a blast of directories. Not bought links. Not comments on strangers’ blogs.
5. **Keep it from rotting.** Hours change. Services change. Google re-crawls. A light admin console and a small monthly SEO habit beat a once-a-year panic.

Technical foundations still matter: mobile layout, speed, image compression, alt text that describes the picture, a sitemap, no accidental `noindex` on the pages you care about. They do not replace a clear offer.

Local SEO in New Zealand is mostly: a truthful Google Business Profile (only if the business is real), a site that names the suburbs you actually cover, and reviews you did not buy. Nationwide remote work is different. You do not pretend to have a shopfront in five cities.

What we will not do, even if asked:

- Guarantee rankings, traffic numbers, or “#1 in Auckland”.
- Stuff keywords into titles, alt text, or hidden copy.
- Spin 40 blog posts that nobody asked for.
- Build private blog networks or buy link packages.
- Create a Google Business Profile for a brand with no genuine location or service area.

Our SEO starter is scoped as technical setup plus light monthly content and keyword work. That is a foundation and a habit. It is not a magic campaign, and we will not dress it up as one.

## NZ notes (.co.nz, enquiry forms, Google Business Profile only if real)

A few New Zealand specifics that generic US blog posts get wrong.

**.co.nz vs .com.** A `.co.nz` domain tells people (and search) you are aiming at New Zealand. It is a clarity thing, not a ranking cheat code. If you already have a `.com` you like, keep it. You can run both and point them at the same site. Registering ten unused `.co.nz` names will not help you.

**Enquiry, not inquiry.** On buttons, forms, and this page we use NZ English: enquiry, colour, organised, specialised. Small, but it is how you sound when you sound local.

**GST.** Prices on [mybrandstudio.club](https://mybrandstudio.club) are in NZD. NZ GST is 15%. Whether a listed figure includes GST is confirmed on the invoice and on the call. Do not compare a US “from $99” template to a NZ invoice without checking tax.

**Hours and phone.** Put hours in NZ time. Put a +64 number if you want calls from here. If you are nationwide remote, say so. If you are Island Bay only, say that too. Vague “serving the region” copy helps no one.

**Google Business Profile only if real.** A GBP is for a business people can actually visit, or a genuine service-area business that goes to customers. It is not a ranking sticker for a purely invented shopfront. If you work from a spare room and never see clients there, do not pin a fake cafe. If you are a tradie with a van and a real service area, a profile can be the difference between a call and a miss. We will help you set one up when it fits Google’s rules. We will not manufacture one.

## FAQ

### Do I need a website if I already have Instagram?

If you want search, trust, and enquiries from people who do not follow you yet, yes. Keep Instagram for personality and proof. Put hours, services, and an enquiry form on a site you own, and point the bio at that.

### Should I build a website or an app first?

Website first. An app is for repeat use: logins, bookings, points, a hub. Almost every cafe, tradie, clinic, and creator still needs a public page so people can find them and understand the offer. You can add a login-ready shell later without starting in the App Store.

### Is a Linktree or Facebook page enough?

They are extras, not a home. A Linktree is a list of buttons. A Facebook page lives on Facebook. Neither gives you crawlable service pages, a proper enquiry form, or a URL that looks serious on an invoice. Keep them. Do not stop there.

### What does a good enough website look like in 2026?

Fast on a phone, honest about what you do, easy to contact, and possible to edit. One clear job on the home page, real photos, HTTPS, a title that matches the search, and a 40 to 60 word answer at the top of important pages. A one-page site can be enough. A 40-page site with no enquiry path is not.

### Should I DIY, hire an agency, or use Brand Studio?

DIY if you have time and patience. An agency if you want a large custom production and you are ready for those rates. My Brand Studio if you want done-for-you from a look that already feels like a shop, a same-day sketch, optional admin so you own the edits, and care plans that cost less than a typical retainer. We are not a free builder.

### How does SEO actually work for a new site?

You publish a useful, crawlable page for a real question, keep the business facts consistent, and let time and genuine mentions do some of the work. Technical setup helps. Hacks do not. New sites are slow. Anyone selling guaranteed rankings is not someone we will try to out-promise.

### What do you actually automate?

Lead routing, follow-ups, workflow bots, and a light admin console if you want to edit hours yourself. Optional social drafts through Brand Studio Capture. We do not automate fake reviews, directory spam, or AI blog farms.

### Do I need a .co.nz domain and a Google Business Profile?

A `.co.nz` is a good idea if your buyers are here, but it is not magic. A Google Business Profile is only for a real location or a genuine service-area business. Do not create one to look bigger than you are.
